How to Drive in Japan: Traffic Rules, Left-Hand Driving, Stop Signs and Parking

Driving in Japan is calm and well organised, but a handful of rules are the opposite of what many visitors are used to. This guide covers the Japan traffic rules that matter most for tourists renting a car in Okinawa, Hokkaido, Kyushu or anywhere else, then lets you practise them in a free 3D driving simulator that runs in your browser.

30-second summary: 7 things to remember when driving in Japan

  1. Drive on the left. Steering wheel and turn-signal stalk are on the right.
  2. Left turns are tight, right turns are wide. For a right turn, move to the centre of the intersection and wait for oncoming traffic.
  3. Red means no movement at all. No turn on red in either direction. Only a green arrow lets you go.
  4. Stop completely at Tomare (止まれ) signs and before every railway crossing.
  5. Pedestrians first. Stop if anyone is on or about to enter a crossing.
  6. No street parking. Use coin parking or designated lots.
  7. Carry IDP + home licence + passport at all times.

1. Documents: International Driving Permit (IDP)

Japan recognises the 1949 Geneva Convention International Driving Permit. You must obtain it in your home country before you travel and carry it together with your home driving licence and passport. Rental companies will refuse the car without all three.

  • Issued by your national motoring body or licensing authority (for example AAA in the US, the Post Office in the UK, CAA in Canada, Australia Post or state motoring clubs in Australia). Usually same-day.
  • Valid for one year from issue, and only while your home licence is valid.
  • Licences from Germany, France, Switzerland, Belgium, Monaco, Taiwan, Slovenia and Estonia are not covered by the Geneva Convention IDP. Drivers from these countries need an official Japanese translation of their licence (from JAF or their embassy) carried with the original licence and passport.
  • Residents staying longer than one year must convert to a Japanese licence.

2. Left-hand traffic and the right-hand driver's seat

Japan drives on the left, so you sit on the right side of the car. Most visitors from right-hand-traffic countries drift too far left and scrape the left wheels on the kerb or clip the left mirror in narrow streets.

Adaptation tips
  • Before leaving the rental lot, drive a few slow laps to learn where the left side of the car is.
  • Keep the centre line close to your right shoulder. If it feels far away, you are too far left.
  • Turn signals are on the right stalk, wipers on the left. If the wipers start, do not panic. Just use the other stalk.
  • Look both ways one extra time at junctions. Traffic approaches from the opposite side to what your instincts expect.

3. Tight left turns, wide right turns

In left-hand traffic the turning geometry is mirrored. Getting this right is half of driving in Japan.

TurnHow to do it in JapanCommon mistake
Left turn (near side)Hug the corner and turn tight straight into the left lane.Swinging wide into the oncoming lane.
Right turn (far side)Enter the intersection, wait near the centre for oncoming traffic to clear, then turn wide into the left lane of the new road.Cutting the corner across oncoming traffic.

Waiting in the middle of the intersection for a right turn is normal and expected. Signal at least 30 m before turning.

4. Traffic signals: no turns on red

This is the rule visitors from North America break most often. In Japan a red light prohibits every movement: no left turn (the near-side turn that would be "right on red" elsewhere), no right turn, no straight.

SignalMeaning
🟢 Solid greenLeft, straight and right all allowed. Yield to oncoming traffic when turning right. U-turns allowed unless signed otherwise.
🟡 YellowStop at the line if you can do so safely.
🔴 RedStop. No turns of any kind.
🔴 + 🟢 arrowsRed, but you may go only in the arrow directions. "← ↑" means left and straight only; "→" means right turn only. Common at large urban intersections.

Japanese signals are mounted horizontally (green, yellow, red from left to right) and usually hang on the far side of the intersection, so they are easy to see from the stop line. Locals call the green light "blue" (ao), and some lamps do look blue-green.

5. Tomare stop signs and railway crossings

The inverted red triangle reading 止まれ (Tomare) is Japan's stop sign. You must bring the car to a complete stop at the stop line (0 km/h), look both ways, and only then proceed. A rolling stop is a violation and police do enforce it. The word is also painted on the road surface.

You must stop before every railway crossing (fumikiri)
Unless the crossing has its own traffic signal, Japanese law requires a full stop before the barrier, even when it is up, followed by a look and listen before crossing in one movement. Do not change gear or stop on the tracks. This rule does not exist in most countries and is easy to forget.

6. Speed limits and road signs

Ordinary roads (no sign)60 km/hUrban streets are usually posted 40–50 km/h

Okinawa, Ishigaki and other islandsMostly 40–50 km/hFew or no expressways. Relax and enjoy the view
  • Speed limits are shown as a number inside a blue circle. A number with a line underneath is a minimum speed.
  • A yellow centre line means no overtaking. Crossing it to turn right into a shop or driveway is allowed, which is why the car ahead may suddenly stop and signal right. Keep your distance.
  • Seat belts are mandatory in all seats. Children under 6 need a child seat. Handling a phone while driving is a serious offence.
  • Drink-driving: 0.15 mg/L of breath alcohol is a crime, impaired driving is punished more severely, and passengers or hosts who served alcohol can be prosecuted too. Treat it as zero tolerance.

7. Parking: never on the street

Street parking is heavily enforced and fines are steep. Always use a coin parking lot or the shop's designated lot. If you must stop on the roadside, let passengers out and move on immediately.

  • Restaurants without their own lot often validate a nearby partner car park. Drop passengers first and let the driver park to save time.
  • Coin parking: drive in, a lock plate rises under the car; pay at the machine by bay number and the plate lowers.
  • Reverse parking is the norm in Japan.

8. Driving etiquette in Japan

  • Pedestrians have absolute priority at crossings. Stop if someone is waiting to cross.
  • Front windows are untinted, so drivers make eye contact and use hand gestures to yield. Use them.
  • Flash the hazard lights two or three times to say thank you after someone lets you in ("thank-you hazard").
  • Horns are almost never used except to warn of danger.
  • On narrow streets both cars stop and one yields. Slowly.

9. Rental cars, fuel, ETC and accidents

Booking and pick-up

  • Ask for English navigation and an ETC card (automatic expressway tolls). Take full insurance including the NOC (non-operation charge) waiver so an accident costs you nothing extra.
  • Photograph every panel before driving away. Minivans such as the Toyota Alphard are wide. Watch the left side.
  • Navigation works best with the destination's phone number or Mapcode.

Fuel

  • Most rentals take regular petrol (レギュラー, red nozzle). Diesel is 軽油 (green), premium is ハイオク (yellow).
  • Return the car full. Ask for or select 満タン (mantan, "full tank") and keep the receipt.

Accidents and breakdowns

  • Police 110, ambulance and fire 119. Even a minor scrape must be reported to the police for insurance to apply.
  • Keep the rental company's emergency number and JAF (Japan Automobile Federation) roadside assistance handy.

Frequently asked questions

Can I drive in Japan with my home country licence?

Not on its own. Most visitors need a 1949 Geneva Convention International Driving Permit plus their home licence and passport. Drivers from Germany, France, Switzerland, Belgium, Monaco, Taiwan, Slovenia and Estonia need an official Japanese translation instead. Get the IDP before you travel.

Can you turn on a red light in Japan?

No. Red prohibits every movement, including the near-side left turn. Only a lit green arrow allows movement in that direction.

Which side of the road does Japan drive on?

The left, with the steering wheel on the right. Left turns are tight and right turns are wide.

What does the Tomare sign mean?

It is the stop sign. Come to a complete stop at the line, look both ways, then go. Rolling through is a ticketable offence.

Do I have to stop at railway crossings?

Yes, before every crossing without its own traffic signal, even when the barrier is up.

What are the speed limits in Japan?

60 km/h on ordinary roads and 100 km/h on expressways unless posted otherwise. Cities and islands are mostly 40–50 km/h.

Is a U-turn allowed in Japan?

Yes on green where there is no no-U-turn sign. Where the sign is posted, no.

Can I park on the street?

Avoid it. Enforcement is strict. Use coin parking or designated lots.

What is the drink-driving limit?

0.15 mg/L of breath alcohol is already a crime, and passengers or hosts can be punished too. Do not drink at all if you are driving.

Can I practise before my trip?
